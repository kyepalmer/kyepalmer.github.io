Families provide an important context for children's emotional development, and amongst family members, children often spend most of their time with their siblings. Sibling relationships play a unique role in modelling and learning emotions through shared experiences, play, and conflict. In the current study, we investigate siblings' recognition of emotional expressions during a structured interactive task. Parent-child-child triads in Australia (N = 44; children aged 4-13 years; predominantly White; data collected 2024-25) took turns producing facial expressions in response to emotion-eliciting objects while their family members guessed the object. Children also watched videos of unfamiliar children completing the same task and guessed which objects they had seen. Parents were more accurate than younger siblings at recognising children's expressions, but did not differ from older siblings, suggesting recognition ability approaches adult-level performance by later childhood. Siblings were more accurate interpreting each other's expressions than those of unfamiliar children, particularly for sad and fear. Children's accuracy improved with age, but sibling age gap did not predict accuracy, suggesting a developmental component to this ability rather than differences in experience between siblings. These findings suggest that familiarity within family relationships plays a meaningful role in emotional understanding, which continues to develop throughout childhood.
